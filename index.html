Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Sir Herbert Brewer (1865–1928) “Orchestral miniatures from a cathedral organist” Sir Alfred Herbert Brewer’s name belongs at the heart of Gloucester’s musical history. Born in the city in 1865, he was a cathedral chorister before studying at Oxford and the Royal College of Music. After appointments in Bristol, Coventry and Tonbridge, he returned to Gloucester Cathedral as organist and choirmaster in 1896, remaining until his death in 1928. As a conductor at the Three Choirs Festival, Brewer helped bring new music before provincial audiences. His own Emmaus was performed at the Gloucester Festival in 1901; its manuscript acknowledges Elgar’s help with the orchestration when pressure of work prevented Brewer from completing the task himself. His teaching reached a remarkable group of young musicians, including Herbert Howells, Ivor Gurney and Ivor Novello. Yet the teacher and cathedral organist should not eclipse the composer. Francis Cunningham Woods(1862-1929) “A string suite named after a Norfolk village”

- October 22, 2026
Francis Cunningham Woods (1862–1929) “A string suite named after a Norfolk village” Francis Cunningham Woods was born in London in 1862. He studied at the National Training School under Arthur Sullivan and Ebenezer Prout before continuing his education at Oxford. Organ appointments at Brasenose and Exeter Colleges placed him in the university’s musical life, and he later served as organist to the Duke of Marlborough. From 1896 he taught at Highgate School. His activities also included conducting the Finsbury Choral Association and composing services, anthems, songs and orchestral music. His theatre work included music for Oxford productions of Shakespeare. With John Stainer he edited The Village Organist , the practical collection of organ music first published by Novello in 1897. That familiar church-music association can obscure the breadth of his other work. The Gressenhall Suite offers a glimpse of Woods beyond the organ loft. William Henry Speer(1863-1937) “A pupil of Stanford and neighbour of Elgar”

- October 22, 2026
William Henry Speer (1863–1937) “A pupil of Stanford and neighbour of Elgar” William Henry Speer was born in London in 1863. He studied with Charles Harford Lloyd at Gloucester and later at the Royal College of Music, where his teachers included Walter Parratt for organ and Charles Villiers Stanford for composition. He also studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, taking musical degrees and eventually a doctorate in 1906. His working life included church appointments, notably at Bexhill from 1903, but his output extended into the concert hall. A contemporary biographical account records performances of his Rhapsody, Festival Overture and Symphony at the Bournemouth Symphony Concerts. His choral ballad The Jackdaw of Rheims was also widely performed. Speer’s catalogue embraces orchestral and chamber music as well as songs, organ pieces and church works. Percy Sherwood(1866-1939) “A symphonist caught between two countries”

- October 22, 2026
Percy Sherwood (1866–1939) “A symphonist caught between two countries” Percy Sherwood was born in Dresden in 1866 to an English father and a German mother. His musical education brought him into contact with Theodor Kirchner, Felix Draeseke and Hermann Scholtz. In 1889 his Requiem won the Mendelssohn Prize, and he established a reputation in Germany as a pianist, composer and teacher. He taught at the Dresden Conservatory, where his pupils included Dora Pejačević. In 1914 he settled in England with his wife, leaving behind the musical world in which he had made his name. Private teaching became an important source of income, but his German reputation did not translate into a comparable place in British concert life. He died in London in 1939. Sherwood’s orchestral ambitions were substantial: symphonies, concertos and serenades stand alongside a large output of chamber and piano music. Many manuscripts survive in Oxford’s Bodleian Library. - October 22, 2026
William “Billy” Reed (1875–1942) “Elgar’s trusted violinist, a composer in his own right” William Henry Reed, known to his friends as Billy, is remembered above all for his close friendship with Edward Elgar. Born in Frome, Somerset, he trained at the Royal Academy of Music and became a founding member of the London Symphony Orchestra in 1904. He led the orchestra from 1912 to 1935 and also taught at the Royal College of Music. Reed’s practical knowledge of the violin was invaluable to Elgar during the composition of the Violin Concerto in 1910. He tried passages, suggested bowings and fingerings, and helped the composer hear how the solo writing would work. His memoir Elgar as I Knew Him (1936) preserves a personal account of their friendship, alongside material relating to the unfinished Third Symphony. - October 22, 2026
Percy Pitt (1869–1932) “A life on the podium, a composer in the shadows” Percy Pitt helped shape British musical life from the concert platform, the opera house and the broadcasting studio. Born in London in 1869, he trained in Leipzig with Reinecke and Jadassohn and continued his studies with Rheinberger in Munich. Returning to London in 1893, he became closely associated with Queen’s Hall, working as pianist, organist and accompanist. Opera increasingly occupied his working life. At Covent Garden he served as musical adviser and assistant conductor before becoming Director of Music in 1907. He later worked with the Beecham Opera Company and the British National Opera Company. His move into broadcasting brought another influential appointment: he became the BBC’s full-time Director of Music in 1924 and remained until 1930. R. O. Morris(1886-1948) “The quiet composer behind the great teacher”

- October 22, 2026
R. O. Morris “Master of counterpoint, forgotten composer” Reginald Owen Morris was born in York in 1886 and educated at Harrow, New College, Oxford, and the Royal College of Music. Known to colleagues and friends simply as R. O., he became an influential teacher of composition and counterpoint. His pupils included Gerald Finzi, Michael Tippett, Constant Lambert and Edmund Rubbra. During the First World War he served in the Duke of Cornwall’s Light Infantry. He returned to the Royal College of Music as a professor in 1920, spending two years at the Curtis Institute in Philadelphia from 1926 before resuming his London teaching. His books, including Contrapuntal Technique in the Sixteenth Century , helped establish his lasting reputation. Morris’s own music has received far less attention. His output includes symphonies, concertante works and music for strings, much of it written during a concentrated period in the 1920s and early 1930s. - October 22, 2026
Charles Oberthür “Orchestral scores amidst a sea of music for the harp” Charles Oberthür was born in Munich in 1819 and made London his home in 1844. His career began in continental theatre orchestras, with engagements in Zürich, Wiesbaden and Mannheim, before he moved to England. In London he initially played at the Italian Opera, then concentrated on teaching, composition and appearances as a soloist. He became a professor of harp at the Royal Academy of Music in 1861. His enormous catalogue is dominated by music for or involving his own instrument, ranging from teaching pieces and arrangements to chamber works and concert music. That reputation can obscure the wider ambitions of a composer who also wrote operatic, choral and orchestral works. Oberthür’s place in this blog rests on his long contribution to musical life in Britain and on the orchestral music that lies beyond his familiar harp repertoire. Born in London in 1858, he studied piano with Walter Macfarren at the Royal Academy of Music, and composition with William Sterndale Bennett and Arthur Sullivan. The connection with Macfarren provides a direct link with another composer explored in this blog. Arthur Hervey(1855-1922) “Writer, critic, promoter and composer”

- October 21, 2026
Arthur Hervey “Writer, critic, promoter and composer” Hervey was born in Paris to Irish parents who were rich landowners in Ireland. Their wealth  facilitated their travels across Europe and they enjoyed cultural pursuits such as music and literature. This privileged background shaped Hervey's formative years before the family relocated to England. He was educated at the Oratory, Birmingham, and studied privately in London with Berthold Tours and Edouard Marlois, two organist-composers with a French background. He spent fairly equal time as both a composer and writer, prinipally biographical studies of composers, most of which were devoted to French contemporaries like Saint-Saëns and Bruneau. He was widely regarded as an expert on French music. As music critic for Vanity Fair from 1889 to 1892 and subsequently for The Morning Post from 1892 to 1908, Hervey penned weekly columns and concert reviews that promoted emerging European talent to British audiences.
