Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Walter Macfarren: "In his brother's shadow?"

Walter Macfarren (1826-1905) Walter Macfarren As a composer, Walter Macfarren (1826-1905) was arguably very much in his brother's shadow but we must be aware that he had a substantial reputation of his own. George Alexander Macfarren His elder brother, Sir George Alexander Macfarren (20 years his senior), held the more prominent public positions: Principal of the Royal Academy of Music and Professor of Music at Cambridge. That along with his large output of orchestral music including 9 symphonies, helps explain why George’s name dominated contemporary musical life.  Walter’s distinction lay particularly in piano teaching, performing and editing. He taught at the Academy from 1846 to 1903, and his pupils included Tobias Matthay and Henry Wood. He was a respected musician, rather than simply an overlooked younger brother.  Orchestral works Only those in bold have surviving scores. Sliding into obscurity

Why did so much music fall into obscurity. The answer to this question is obviously more complicated than simply saying 'It wasn't good enough'. We need to learn a bit more about late Victorian and Edwardian British musical culture. Provincial music festivals were a welcome force for commissioning new British music, both orchestral and choral. Cities such as Cardiff, Norwich, Leeds, and Birmingham provided composers with a decent  orchestra and nationwide press coverage. The Philharmonic Society, Queens Hall concerts under the direction of Henry Wood and Dan Godfrey's orchestra in Bournemouth also premiered new British music. However, once a composition completed its debut it was more often than not set aside to clear the schedule for the next batch of commissions.   This was no more evident than in Bournemouth where Dan Godfrey largely created what has been termed the ‘Bournemouth Problem’.
