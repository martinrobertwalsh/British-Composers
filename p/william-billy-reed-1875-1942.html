Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores from various sources I have hopefully created decent renditions of their music using Sibelius and Noteperformer software.

We can at last hear their music brought back to life; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

William “Billy” Reed (1875-1942)

“Elgar’s trusted violinist, a composer in his own right”

William Henry Reed, known to his friends as Billy, is remembered above all for his close friendship with Edward Elgar. Born in Frome, Somerset, he trained at the Royal Academy of Music and became a founding member of the London Symphony Orchestra in 1904. He led the orchestra from 1912 to 1935 and also taught at the Royal College of Music.

Reed’s practical knowledge of the violin was invaluable to Elgar during the composition of the Violin Concerto in 1910. He tried passages, suggested bowings and fingerings, and helped the composer hear how the solo writing would work. His memoir Elgar as I Knew Him (1936) preserves a personal account of their friendship, alongside material relating to the unfinished Third Symphony.

William Henry "Billy" Reed (29 July 1876 – 2 July 1942) was an English violinist, teacher, minor composer, conductor and biographer of Sir Edward Elgar. He was leader of the London Symphony Orchestra for 23 years (1912–1935), but is best known for his long personal friendship with Elgar (1910–1934) and his book Elgar As I Knew Him (1936), in which he goes into great detail about the genesis of the Violin Concerto in B minor. The book also provides a large number of Elgar's sketches for his unfinished Third Symphony, which proved invaluable sixty years later when Anthony Payne elaborated and essentially completed the work, although Reed wrote that in his view the symphony could not be completed.

His name appears in various forms: William Henry Reed, W. H. Reed, W. H. "Billy" Reed, Billy Reed and Willie Reed. He was known to his friends as Billy.

His own composing life deserves a separate hearing. Orchestral fantasies, suites, concertante works and chamber music reveal a musician whose interests extended well beyond the violin. He was also a committed conductor of amateur orchestras. His Symphony for Strings was written for the Bromley and Chislehurst Orchestra and dedicated to its leader, Marjorie Whyte.

Biographical references: Bromley Symphony Orchestra centenary history; William Henry Reed. The dates 1875–1942 follow his memorial inscription; some older references give 1876.

Somerset Idylls (1926)

Reed’s Somerset background gives an immediate personal resonance to the title of these two orchestral idylls. Written in 1926, they offer a natural starting point for exploring the composer behind the familiar stories of Elgar.

The autograph orchestral score survives at the Royal College of Music as MS 5390. The library has made a complete digital scan available through IMSLP: 70 pages covering both movements. This surviving manuscript allows the work to be studied in its original orchestral form.

VIEW SCORE: Somerset Idylls


"Billy" Reed was an English violinist, teacher, composer, conductor and major biographer of Edward Elgar. He was leader of the London Symphony Orchestra for 23 years (1912–1935)

He studied at the Royal Academy of Music in London under Émile Sauret and Frederick Corder, graduating with honours.

Most of Reed's output is chamber music and orchestral/string music. The use of English folksong pervades a lot of his work. This couldn't be more apparent than in his Somerset Idylls of 1926. There are two movements, The Crystal Spring and May Morning (Seventeen Come Sunday)

The first movement has one theme which is developed after its announcement in the cellos and violas. The whole movement is in the sample below.





SAMPLE: Somerset Idylls - 1st mov 'The Crystal Spring'


May Morning, the second movement uses the english folksong "Seventeen Come Sunday". This was used by Vaughan-Williams in the first movement of his English Folk Song Suite in 1923. In fact it was first used by  Percy Grainger for choir and brass accompaniment in 1912.

Reeds arrangement starts of in quite a raucus fashion with the upper woodwind playing the tune over a grinding ground bass.








SAMPLE: Somerset Idylls - 2nd mov 'May Morning' - start


The music calms down and we hear the main theme on a solo horn before a recapitulation begins, but this time a counter melody intertwines with the main theme.
