Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores from various sources I have hopefully created decent renditions of their music using Sibelius and Noteperformer software.

We can at last hear their music brought back to life; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Sliding into the shadows

Why did so much music fall into obscurity. The answer to this question is obviously more complicated than simply saying 'It wasn't good enough'. We need to learn a bit more about late Victorian and Edwardian British musical culture.

Provincial music festivals were a welcome force for commissioning new British music, both orchestral and choral. Cities such as Cardiff, Norwich, Leeds, and Birmingham provided composers with a decent  orchestra and nationwide press coverage. The Philharmonic Society, Queens Hall concerts under the direction of Henry Wood and Dan Godfrey's orchestra in Bournemouth also premiered new British music. However, once a composition completed its debut it was more often than not set aside to clear the schedule for the next batch of commissions.  

This was no more evident than in Bournemouth where Dan Godfrey largely created what has been termed the ‘Bournemouth Problem’. His enthusiasm for British music caused him to encourage a massive range of composing talents. His programmes were full of music by up and coming composers, one hit wonders and established talent. Most likely there were a few ‘has beens’ as well. However relatively few pieces ‘stuck’ in the repertoire and most of these ‘novelties’ after a couple of performances were put to one side and quietly lost. Unfortunately that often included the score and parts as well; works that will not and perhaps more disturbingly, cannot be recovered.

If the composer had the initiative, influence and the right contacts he might get further performances around the country. It's worth looking at an example; The Symphony in Bb by Walter Macfarren completed in August 1879. The work is conservative in style for its time, a mix of Mendelssohn and Mozart as one critic described it. Despite this it received a number of performances in some surprising places. These are noted, with dates, on the inside cover of the score. (click on image to enlarge)

For a relatively 'minor' composer this was impressive to say the least and certainly not typical. It was even revived after a ten year gap just two years before Macfarren died.  However, the score then went the way of so many and seemed to simply disappear, presumed lost until I was lucky enough to discover it lurking in the library of North-Western University, Illinois!

For the aspiring composer there were also commercial and publication barriers to overcome.  A significant portion of Edwardian orchestral music, was never formally published in full orchestral score or parts. Music publishers like Novello & Company focused their capital primarily on vocal scores for choral societies or short piano arrangements, which offered guaranteed retail markets. Works surviving solely in manuscript score and handwritten orchestral parts were exceptionally vulnerable to physical loss, damage, or total neglect once the original performance material was archived.

Following the trauma of the First World War, British musical culture turned away from Victorian and Edwardian descriptive tone painting, favoring initially the nationalistic pastoralism of Ralph Vaughan Williams. A swathe of lesser known composers jumped on  this bandwagon and to be fair some very worthwhile music was written.  It was probably the over-reliance on modal melody and harmony that prompted Elisabeth Lutyens (an ardent modernist composing in twelve-tone serialism) to coin the disparaging term "cowpat music".

We are fortunate that many unpublished scores do still survive, collecting dust in various libraries across the British Isles. But for every one that still exisits there are undoubtedly many more lost or hidden away in a relatives attic.

By way of conclusion I'd like to bring to your attention the name of Thomas Burton (1842-1935) composer and organist. Most of his orchestral music was performed under Dan Godfrey. Here is his work list:

It's a mouthwatering array of  music but sadly none of it has survived. You'd have thought that one or two pieces would be in a library somewhere but no, not a single note has survived. As a composer Burton was held in high esteem as his obituary states:

'Mr. Burton was an English composer of the first order, but owing to his modest and retiring disposition, he was not so widely recognised as he should have been'

Like many from his era and before, they have all, quite simply, "Gone for a Burton"
