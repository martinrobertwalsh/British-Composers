Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores from various sources I have hopefully created decent renditions of their music using Sibelius and Noteperformer software.

We can at last hear their music brought back to life; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Arthur Hervey (1855-1922)

“Writer, critic, promoter and composer”

Hervey was born in Paris to Irish parents who were rich landowners in Ireland. Their wealth  facilitated their travels across Europe and they enjoyed cultural pursuits such as music and literature. This privileged background shaped Hervey's formative years before the family relocated to England.

He was educated at the Oratory, Birmingham, and studied privately in London with Berthold Tours and Edouard Marlois, two organist-composers with a French background.

He spent fairly equal time as both a composer and writer, prinipally biographical studies of composers, most of which were devoted to French contemporaries like Saint-Saëns and Bruneau. He was widely regarded as an expert on French music. As music critic for Vanity Fair from 1889 to 1892 and subsequently for The Morning Post from 1892 to 1908, Hervey penned weekly columns and concert reviews that promoted emerging European talent to British audiences.

Hervey Plaque

His stepdaughter, Nancy Webster, left a bequest to the Royal Academy of Music in 1959 even though Hervey had no official connection to the college. The academy uses this funding to award the annual Arthur Hervey Scholarship (or Prize) to support outstanding young composition and performance students during their studies. A carved marble relief portrait celebrating his legacy was donated to the RAM collection and is kept in the Royal Academy of Music Museum.

As a composer he wrote quite extensively for orchestra, mainly in the genre of tone poems.

YOUTH -  A CONCERT OVERTURE (1902)

Below are Hervey's programme notes from the start of the score:


Enthusiasm, exuberance of spirit, martial ardour, and, above all, love, are the characteristics of that period of our being termed youth, when everything seems bright and hopeful, the sorrows that come to all men not having as yet appeared to cast their shadows over life and rob it of its illusions.

This happy period has suggested the present work which, it will be seen, is conceived in an optimistic vein, the few clouds that threaten to mar the prevailing brightness soon being dispelled.

The Overture commences with the following theme, both sections of which are much employed throughout the work, played fortissimo. We may imagine a young man walking out on a bright spring day in buoyant spirits, a thousand varied thoughts of possible achievements coursing through his brain.


The above subject is repeated in the key of A flat, and after some modulations returns to the initial key and leads to the following march tune—

This gradually works up to a climax, then little by little becomes subdued.

The following theme, played by the wood-wind to a humming accompaniment of violas, is intended to suggest a girl at her spinning-wheel—

The spinning is rather suddenly interrupted, and the succeeding Allegro, No. 4, in which use is also made of Theme No. 1, B, suggests agitation of mind—

The agitation subsides by degrees, and it may be presumed that the girl is induced to listen to a declaration of love expressed by the following theme—

The Overture is largely made up of the above thematic material. The themes are employed now singly, now jointly, and in their various guises will doubtless be easily identified.

The youth’s advances are evidently but half-heartedly repelled by the maiden, and after once more singing his passionate song of love, he proceeds on his way, his heart overflowing with joy, while the march theme, No. 2, is played by the entire orchestra, and the work concludes with a brilliant peroration, in which figure the chief themes.

The Overture is scored for a full orchestra, including, besides the usual strings and wind instruments, a cor anglais, a bass clarinet, a double bassoon, harp, and side drum.

A. H.
