Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores from various sources I have hopefully created decent renditions of their music using Sibelius and Noteperformer software.

We can at last hear their music brought back to life; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Percy Sherwood (1866-1939)

“A symphonist caught between two countries”

Percy Sherwood was born in Dresden in 1866 to an English father and a German mother. His musical education brought him into contact with Theodor Kirchner, Felix Draeseke and Hermann Scholtz. In 1889 his Requiem won the Mendelssohn Prize, and he established a reputation in Germany as a pianist, composer and teacher.

He taught at the Dresden Conservatory, where his pupils included Dora Pejačević. In 1914 he settled in England with his wife, leaving behind the musical world in which he had made his name. Private teaching became an important source of income, but his German reputation did not translate into a comparable place in British concert life. He died in London in 1939.

Sherwood’s orchestral ambitions were substantial: symphonies, concertos and serenades stand alongside a large output of chamber and piano music. Many manuscripts survive in Oxford’s Bodleian Library. The Second Symphony provides a particularly intriguing example of a major work that remained outside the established repertory.

Biographical reference: Joseph Spooner’s notes for Toccata Classics.

Symphony No.2 in B minor (1892)

Sherwood completed this symphony in Dresden in 1892, when he was only twenty-six. Its autograph score survives in the Bodleian Library as MS. Mus. c.411, and bears a dedication to Miss Charlotte C. Whittle.

The four movements follow a substantial Romantic plan. A slow introduction opens the first movement; the Adagio leads directly into a fast Scherzo with two trios. The finale is marked Allegro con fuoco and concludes with a Maestoso. The manuscript dates the completion of the finale to 30 December 1892.

  1. Adagio molto – Allegro molto
  2. Adagio
  3. Scherzo: Prestissimo – Trio I – Trio II
  4. Finale: Allegro con fuoco – Maestoso

Alongside the usual woodwind, brass and strings, the score calls for cor anglais, contrabassoon, bass drum and cymbals. This is orchestral writing on a broad scale, and a reminder that Sherwood’s surviving manuscripts contain considerably more than the piano miniatures published during his lifetime.

Manuscript description: Jürgen Schaarwächter, Two Centuries of British Symphonism, volume II, p.989.
