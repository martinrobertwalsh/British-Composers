Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Walter Macfarren: "In his brother's shadow?"

- October 14, 2026

Walter Macfarren (1826-1905)

As a composer, Walter Macfarren (1826-1905) was arguably very much in his brother's shadow but we must be aware that he had a substantial reputation of his own.

His elder brother, Sir George Alexander Macfarren (20 years his senior), held the more prominent public positions: Principal of the Royal Academy of Music and Professor of Music at Cambridge. That along with his large output of orchestral music including 9 symphonies, helps explain why George’s name dominated contemporary musical life. 


Walter’s distinction lay particularly in piano teaching, performing and editing. He taught at the Academy from 1846 to 1903, and his pupils included Tobias Matthay and Henry Wood. He was a respected musician, rather than simply an overlooked younger brother.

SYMPHONY IN Bb 

Last heard in 1902

This was presumed to be lost but I was fortunate enoguh to discover an autograph score residing in the archives of the library of North-Western University, Illinois!

Completed in August 1879 the work is conservative in style for its time, a mix of Mendelssohn and Mozart as one critic described it*. Despite this it received a number of performances in some surprising places. These are noted, with dates, on the inside cover of the score. (click on image to enlarge)

For a relatively 'minor' composer this record of performance was impressive to say the least but certainly not typical. It was even revived after a ten year gap just two years before Macfarren died.  

It's in the the standard four movements as follows:

1. Andante Lento : Allegro assai
2. Andante poco Allegretto
3. Scherzo: Allegretto Leggiero
4. Andante Lento: Allegro con Maesta

Walter in his autobiography recounts an enthusiastic reception at the premier in Brighton:


On the 5th of February, 1880, my overture, " Hero and Leander," was given at the Philharmonic as before mentioned, and my Symphony in B flat was produced at Kuhe's Brighton Festival on the 24th of the same month, and hence it has been nicknamed "The Brighton Symphony." Many of the orchestral professors engaged at the Academy were likewise in Mr. Kuhe's Band, and others, as well as the leading students, volunteered to go down to Brighton, and thus increase the orchestra in support of their conductor, and the occasion was marked by great enthusiasm. My dinner-party at the "Old Ship" (where I was located) was augmented by the arrival of my dear brother G. A. Macfarren, accompanied by his then secretary, the late Windyer Clark, both of whom had travelled down expressly to hear the Symphony, and returned the same night.

A reviewer of the symphony described it as kaleidoscopic: 

The merit of this composition is unquestionable, and nothing but a sustained manner of its own is wanting to place it, as a work of high pretension, beyond the pale of criticism. *Mozart, Mendelssohn and Sterndale Bennett, however, continually peeping out, the impression is in a great degree what may be termed kaleidoscopic.... Apart from these considerations, the symphony is interesting throughout.

1st movement - Andante Lento : Allegro assai

The slow introduction starts unusually with string pizzicato over sustained notes on the horns and trumpets. The woodwind are then introduced in their own ensemble.





The Allegro then follows with the main subject on strings.
