Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

William Henry Speer (1863-1937)

- October 22, 2026

“A pupil of Stanford and neighbour of Elgar”

William Henry Speer was born in London in 1863. He studied with Charles Harford Lloyd at Gloucester and later at the Royal College of Music, where his teachers included Walter Parratt for organ and Charles Villiers Stanford for composition. He also studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, taking musical degrees and eventually a doctorate in 1906.

His working life included church appointments, notably at Bexhill from 1903, but his output extended into the concert hall. A contemporary biographical account records performances of his Rhapsody, Festival Overture and Symphony at the Bournemouth Symphony Concerts. His choral ballad The Jackdaw of Rheims was also widely performed.

Speer’s catalogue embraces orchestral and chamber music as well as songs, organ pieces and church works. Today much of this music awaits renewed attention. His surviving scores offer a firmer starting point than his present obscurity might suggest.

Biographical reference: Grove’s Dictionary of Music and Musicians, supplementary entry, p.667.

Rhapsody in E-flat

The Rhapsody in E-flat belongs to Speer’s orchestral output heard at Bournemouth during his lifetime. Its composition date is not securely established here.

In December 2015, Speer’s granddaughter Caroline Hart described owning a bound printed copy of its full orchestral score. Her account is valuable evidence that the piece survived in orchestral form, rather than only as a title in a worklist. The publisher and current location of that copy still need to be established.

This is a work whose recovery for performance should begin with the score itself. A detailed musical account can follow once that material is available for study.

Sources: the contemporary Grove entry linked above and Caroline Hart’s first-hand account of the score.
