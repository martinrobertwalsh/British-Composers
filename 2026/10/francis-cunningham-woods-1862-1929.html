Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Francis Cunningham Woods (1862-1929)

- October 22, 2026

“From the organ loft to a small village in Norfolk”

Francis Cunningham Woods was born in London in 1862. He studied at the National Training School under Arthur Sullivan and Ebenezer Prout before continuing his education at Oxford. Organ appointments at Brasenose and Exeter Colleges placed him in the university’s musical life, and he later served as organist to the Duke of Marlborough.

From 1896 he taught at Highgate School. His activities also included conducting the Finsbury Choral Association and composing services, anthems, songs and orchestral music. His theatre work included music for Oxford productions of Shakespeare.

With John Stainer he edited The Village Organist, the practical collection of organ music first published by Novello in 1897. That familiar church-music association can obscure the breadth of his other work. The Gressenhall Suite offers a glimpse of Woods beyond the organ loft.

References: Francis Cunningham Woods; Sibley Music Library: The Village Organist.

The Gressenhall Suite (1915)

Published by Hawkes & Son in 1915, The Gressenhall Suite is scored for strings and piano. A library union catalogue records a duration of approximately ten minutes and identifies surviving piano-conductor and instrumental parts, rather than merely a piano arrangement.

A New Zealand radio-programme notice in 1927 lists four movements:

  1. Preamble
  2. Norfolk Folk Tune
  3. Slow Air
  4. Jig

Those titles suggest a compact sequence in which a local folk tune and a concluding dance stand beside a slower lyrical movement. The broadcast notice also shows that the suite travelled beyond Britain and remained in circulation more than a decade after publication.

Here is a manageable work for a small ensemble, with documented surviving parts: a practical prospect for rediscovery as well as a reminder of Woods’s lighter orchestral writing.

Performing material: IAML Australia, Union Catalogue of Orchestral Scores and Performing Parts, p.455. Movement titles and broadcast notice: Radio Record, 16 December 1927, p.7.
