Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Percy Godfrey (1859-1945)

- October 20, 2026
LISTEN: Coronation March

“Prizewinner and a slide from the limelight.”

Godfrey began his studies at the University of Durham and then at the Royal Academy of Music, where his teachers included Sir George Macfarren and Ebenezer Prout. The Dutch violinist and composer Berthold Tours, who became a close friend, was also an important early influence.

After graduating he became a schoolmaster in Bengeo, Hertford before being appointed music-master and organist at The King's School, Canterbury. He conducted the East Kent Orchestra.

THE CORONATION PRIZE MARCH (1901)

If one single piece of music brought Percy Godfrey instant fame it was his Coronation March.

The Worshipful Company of Musicians (one of the Livery Companies of the City of London) held a competition for a new March to be played during the Coronation festivities of King Edward VII and Queen Alexandra the following year. A prize of 50 guineas was to be awarded to the winner and the adjudicators were Sir Frederick Bridge, Sir Walter Parratt and Sir Hubert Parry. There were 189 competitors and the eventual winner was our Percy himself. 

Sales of the march along with a special Coronation Concert at the Royal Albert Hall where nine massed bands performed raised over £3000 for the King Edward's Hospital fund. Silk square replicas of the score also helped raise money for the charity fund.
 

Like most works in this genre the music is in an ABA shape. The principal march material is stately and memorable, the overall feel in this 5 minute offering is one of happiness and joy.

This is one reason the piece worked so well beyond its original occasion: its melodic construction was sufficiently straightforward to be readily appreciated by a large audience.

The triumphant melody after the initial short fanfare.


The Trio section provides the expected contrast with a slower more lyrical mood.  It was was adapted as a hymn tune by Sir Frederick Bridge no less.
 
The coda is particularly rousing.
