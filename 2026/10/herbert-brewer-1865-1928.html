Forgotten Composers British Orchestral Music 1850-1950

Welcome

Welcome to this blog, which explores British orchestral music written between 1850 and 1950. My focus is on composers whose work has sadly fallen into oblivion. By searching for orchestral scores across a multitude of sources and inputting many notes using Sibelius and Noteperformer software I have created high quality renditions of their work so we can at last hear the actual music as it was intended; music which has for too long been forgotten, and in some cases never performed.

Herbert Brewer (1865-1928)

- October 22, 2026

“Orchestral miniatures from a cathedral organist”

Sir Alfred Herbert Brewer’s name belongs at the heart of Gloucester’s musical history. Born in the city in 1865, he was a cathedral chorister before studying at Oxford and the Royal College of Music. After appointments in Bristol, Coventry and Tonbridge, he returned to Gloucester Cathedral as organist and choirmaster in 1896, remaining until his death in 1928.

As a conductor at the Three Choirs Festival, Brewer helped bring new music before provincial audiences. His own Emmaus was performed at the Gloucester Festival in 1901; its manuscript acknowledges Elgar’s help with the orchestration when pressure of work prevented Brewer from completing the task himself.

His teaching reached a remarkable group of young musicians, including Herbert Howells, Ivor Gurney and Ivor Novello. Yet the teacher and cathedral organist should not eclipse the composer. Alongside services and anthems, Brewer wrote orchestral pieces, secular choral music and songs. The brief Elegy offers an intimate introduction to that creative voice.

References: Baker’s biographical entry; Gloucestershire Archives; Simon Carpenter’s research on Brewer and his pupils.

Elegy (published 1916)

Brewer’s Elegy was published by Augener in 1916 as an organ piece. The surviving first-edition score is available from the Sibley Music Library and through IMSLP. It is important to distinguish that original from the string-orchestra version heard here.

Martin Walsh’s digital realisation presents the work for string orchestra. The sustained tone of strings offers a different way of hearing music conceived for the organ: a modern orchestral perspective on Brewer’s original, rather than evidence of an orchestration by the composer.
